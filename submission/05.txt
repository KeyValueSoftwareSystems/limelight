Reaching the first users. Product-led and community-led, with no sales team. The wedge is a free tool that does one useful thing immediately: point it at a track and get a show for the lights you already own. DJs demonstrate gear to each other constantly, so the distribution channel is the users themselves - a before-and-after clip of sound-active mode against a scored show is the entire marketing asset, and it is fifteen seconds long. Beyond that: DJ and lighting subreddits and Discords, YouTube gear reviewers, and the retailers who have an incentive to make inexpensive fixtures look better than they are.

Revenue, in three layers, arriving in this order.

A subscription for the working tool, aimed at people earning money with it. A mobile DJ charges a few hundred per event and spends hours programming; a monthly fee that removes those hours is an easy trade, and it is the pricing shape this segment already accepts.

Freemium for the consumer tier once the smart-light reader ships. Free for a few songs, paid for an unlimited library and offline use.

Licensing the score layer. Companies that ship music-reactive features build and maintain a weaker version of this themselves. Selling them the file, per track or per seat, is the highest-margin layer and the one that makes this a platform rather than an app. It is a later business and nothing here depends on it.

There is a fourth possibility we are deliberately not counting on: labels and artists publishing an official score alongside a release, so the artist's intent travels with the song. It is the most interesting version of this and the slowest to arrange.
