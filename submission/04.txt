Existing alternatives. At the top, lighting consoles - grandMA, Avolites, Chamsys - driven by a human programmer. Excellent, and the cost is the person. In our segment the direct competitor is SoundSwitch: it analyses tracks in advance and auto-scripts lighting, owned by inMusic, and genuinely good. Below that, the sound-active mode built into almost every fixture, and consumer apps such as Philips Hue Sync and Govee's music mode. And the honest baseline: most people do nothing and leave the lights on a preset.

How we differ. SoundSwitch is a lighting product tied to one DJ ecosystem, and what it produces is a lighting artefact - it cannot drive anything that is not a light. We produce a portable description of the music itself, it works with any fixture over open standards, and the same file already drives a reader that has nothing to do with lighting. Against the consumer apps the difference is structural rather than incremental: they react to loudness, so they cannot anticipate anything.

Evidence. Mostly behavioural. People already pay for automated lighting in this segment, which settles willingness to pay. Sound-active mode is the default everywhere and is widely disliked. We have also built and measured the system end to end - a record scored in 17 seconds, real fixtures driven over Art-Net - and one measurement surprised us: the gap between a show that reads as broken and one that reads as deliberate was a brightness contrast of 7.3:1 against 1.4:1 - a constraint we would not have guessed without building it.

What we do not yet have is enough conversations with the people who would pay. That is what we are collecting now.

The biggest assumption that could make this fail is that an audience can perceive anticipation at all. If a room cannot tell the difference between a show that builds into a drop and one that reacts to it, the file is elegant and worthless. Cheap to test: same song, same rig, both versions, ask which one people would rather stand in.
