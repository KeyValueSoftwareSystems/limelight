Every song already has a structure - a tempo, bars, a verse that gives way to a chorus, a build that promises a drop. Musicians read it instantly. Machines do not.

So almost every product that makes something happen "to the music" reacts to loudness instead. Louder means brighter. That approach has a defect it can never fix: it cannot know what is coming. It cannot build into a drop, because a build is only a build if something follows it, and by the time the drop arrives the reaction is already late. The result is the flashing you see in a bar's sound-active mode - technically synchronised, and nothing like what a human lighting designer would do.

The people who care most pay to avoid it. A mobile DJ programs a wedding set by hand, several hours per set. A small venue either hires an operator or leaves the lights on a preset all night. A festival pays a lighting designer. Everyone else accepts the flashing.

Why now. Two things changed. Addressable lighting became cheap and standard - a small Art-Net node speaks the same protocol as a fifty-thousand-pound console, and tens of millions of homes already own colour-controllable lights. And audio analysis stopped needing a data centre: we score a four-minute record in 17 seconds on an ordinary laptop with no GPU, which means the analysis can happen before the track plays rather than during it.

That is the opportunity. If the structure of a song is worked out in advance and written down, anything that responds to music can stop reacting and start performing.
