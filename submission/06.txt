What we can demonstrate at KeyCode. The complete chain, working: a real record analysed on a laptop, a score file produced, an editor for correcting that score by ear, and physical lights performing the song in the room. We have this running now - real fixtures driven over Art-Net - along with a second, non-lighting reader consuming the same file. We can also show the comparison the whole argument rests on: the same song reacting, then anticipating.

Technical risks. Beat and structure detection on difficult material is the real one, because a wrong tempo makes everything downstream wrong. We handle it by grading every field against the recording rather than against our own output, logging every run so we can tell a better result from a stricter test, and letting a human correct a score by ear in a timeline editor when the machine is wrong. Fixtures arriving without documentation is a nuisance rather than a risk - open fixture libraries cover most of it. Timing and latency on real hardware we have already measured.

Business risks. The consumer segment has incumbents who bundle a weaker version for free, which is exactly why we start where people already pay. Music licensing is worth being precise about: we analyse audio the user already has, and we distribute no music, so we are not in a licensing position today. If we later publish a library of pre-computed scores for commercial tracks, that changes, and we would approach it carefully.

Dependencies. None of the difficult kind. No specialised hardware - the product is software and the lights belong to the customer. No significant capital, no manufacturing, no regulatory approval, no inaccessible data, no enterprise sales cycle. It runs on an ordinary laptop with no GPU. A small team can validate this in 90 days by putting it in the hands of thirty DJs and watching whether they keep using it.
