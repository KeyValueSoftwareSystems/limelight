You are building the listener

A machine that hears a piece of music and writes down what it is doing. Everything else in this project is a consumer of that one file.

Limelight · analysis lane · 2 September 2026 · sixteen days to KeyCode

1 · Where your work actually sits

It is easy to look at this project and conclude that we are building a lighting system, and that your job is to feed it. That reading is backwards, and it will make you build the wrong thing.

The product is the understanding. The map file is what understanding looks like when written down, and you are the only person who produces it. Lighting is one reader among many — it is first because it gives feedback in milliseconds and looks extraordinary in a room, which is exactly what a demo needs, and for no other reason. If we deleted the entire lighting lane tomorrow, your work would be unchanged.

The test of whether the machine understood the music is whether a program that has never heard the audio can act on what it wrote. That is the whole specification, and it is why this is an interesting problem rather than a plumbing exercise.

2 · What "understanding" decomposes into

Understanding is not one thing, and the map does not treat it as one. It carries six kinds of fact, and each of them is something a musician would recognise as a separate question about a piece of music.

FieldThe musical questionState of the art
beatsWhere is the pulse? Largely solved. Neural trackers are strong on steady material
downbeatsWhere does the bar begin? A separate, harder problem. Not every fourth beat
chaptersWhat is the form? Where does the character change? Self-similarity over beat-synchronous features. Workable, noisy
momentsWhat happens, and exactly when? Drops are tractable. Stops need the gap measured
spansWhere is it going, and how fast? Effectively unsolved. The interesting one
energyHow intense is it, right now? Easy to fake with loudness. Hard to get right

And then there is everything left over. There is no word for "this feels like the second half of a Coldplay song", and there never will be, so that residue lands in a second tier — vectors, one row per beat, everything the model noticed. It is null today and its slot is already specified, so filling it later changes nothing downstream.

The asymmetry between the two tiers is worth holding on to: the vector is the evidence and the field is the verdict, and only a verdict can be argued with. You cannot hand a person 768 numbers and ask whether they are wrong. Since corrections from real users are the thing this project is ultimately accumulating, the named tier is the asset — not scaffolding to be discarded once the embeddings get good.

3 · The hard part: a trajectory is not an event

Version 0.1 of the map could say a drop happens at 52.0 and could not say it lifts from 1:05 to 1:20. A drop is an instant, so a point describes it exactly. An elevation is a stretch with a direction, and there is no honest way to write it as a point.

This is not an edge case. Opus is one monotonic four-and-a-half-minute riser and almost nothing else; a point-only map cannot express that song at all. So the map gained two fields, and producing them is your most interesting problem, because unlike beat tracking there is no library to call.

Energy is not loudness. A build can get quieter and still lift — a layer arrives, a filter opens, the pitch rises, the density thickens, the snare starts landing on every beat instead of every other one. An RMS curve calls the drop the loudest point and the build flat, which is the exact opposite of the shape a room needs. This is the single most common way to produce a technically valid map that feels wrong.

4 · How you are graded, and by whom

Your output is statistical, which means there is no answer key to hand you — nobody can ship you the correct map for a song the way we shipped the lighting lane its correct frames. What can be made exact is the measurement. So the pack contains a bench rather than an answer.

Start by running the bench's own self-test. It takes one map, breaks it in eleven specific ways, and prints what the bench notices, which is the fastest available way to learn what each metric is blind to before you trust it on something real.

Then read example/expected-report.txt, which is the bench run against a deliberately ordinary first attempt. Four things in it are worth studying before you write any code, because each one is a mistake you would otherwise make yourself:

  1. Beats 0.979, downbeats 0.099. The candidate derived downbeats as beats[::4]. One missing beat shifts the bar phase and it never recovers. Beats and downbeats are different problems with different models.
  2. A stop 350 ms short, flagged audible. Onset accuracy was perfect and the holds value was estimated rather than measured. In a room this reads as a mistake.
  3. One span with IoU 1.00 and the wrong rise. Perfect position, wrong shape — a room that lifts at the wrong time.
  4. Energy r = 0.981, with heavy noise added. Correlation flatters any curve with strong structure. Look at the shape; do not trust the coefficient.

5 · The ladder

We are not building something that understands all music. We are building something that understands one song, then a song with exactly one new hard property, then another. Full table in LEVELS.md.

New difficultySongWhat breaks if you skip ahead
1A rigid grid — constant tempo, 4/4, machine-made The NightsNothing. Calibration: if the bench is not near 1.0 here, the bug is yours
2Long-form trajectory — structure in minutes Opus, StrobeSpan detection. A pop-tuned model finds no boundary at all in a four-minute riser
3Human timing — tempo drifts continuously live rockEvery constant-beat-interval assumption
4A different meter — cycles that are not four beats taal, 7/8Downbeats. "Every fourth beat" stops being a definition
5Deliberate tempo change film scoreThe single global tempo, wherever it is hiding
6Structure without a pulse Raga of Revenge, from DCThe assumption that structure is built on beats at all

All six are measured; three are demoed. A level costs one truth file, an hour of careful listening. A demo costs a week. Track the bench per level — if level 2 improves while you work on level 1, the method transfers and we know the curriculum is real rather than one-song overfitting.

Level 6 is the one that matters for KeyCode and the one we expect to fail. That turns out to be useful: playing a song where the machine scores a third, saying so out loud, correcting two moments by hand and playing it again is a stronger demonstration than any song it gets right.

6 · The six problems

Problem 1 — Emit a legal map

Audio in, a map out that survives the validator and renders without a crash in the lighting reader. Beats, downbeats, chapters. Nothing clever.

Passes
python3 validate.py yours.map.json exits 0 with no errors
Trap
Units. The validator rejects a beat outside the song length precisely because emitting frames or milliseconds is the most common first bug, and it otherwise fails silently three lanes downstream.

Problem 2 — Beat the bench on level 1

Beat F ≥ 0.85 at ±70 ms on The Nights, with no octave warning.

Passes
the number, and the absence of the warning
Trap
A tracker locked to 63 BPM instead of 126 hits every beat it claims and looks perfect to the eye. The bench prints an explicit octave check; it is there because this fools people, repeatedly.

Problem 3 — Downbeats as their own problem

Downbeat F ≥ 0.80. Detect them; do not index into the beat array.

Passes
the number holds even when beat recall is imperfect
Trap
Round chapter boundaries to the nearest downbeat. A boundary between downbeats is almost always a mis-hear, and it makes the room lurch.

Problem 4 — Form: within 3 s, then within 0.5 s

Self-similarity over beat-synchronous features. Get the loose tolerance first; the tight one is a different quality of work.

Passes
boundary F ≥ 0.70 at 3 s, then ≥ 0.50 at 0.5 s
Trap
Over-segmenting is punished — precision is in the F-measure, so twenty proposed boundaries to catch seven real ones scores worse than seven confident ones.

Problem 5 — Spans and energy. The real one.

Detect that a stretch is a build, find its start and end, name its rise shape, and emit an energy point per downbeat. There is no library for this. Nothing downstream can invent it, which is exactly why it is yours.

Passes
span IoU ≥ 0.70 on both builds in The Nights; correct rise on at least one; an energy curve whose shape survives inspection
Trap
Energy is not loudness. Try onset density, spectral centroid, stem count from Demucs, high-frequency content, and harmonic tension — then look at the curve next to the waveform and ask whether it lifts where the music lifts.

Problem 6 — Honest confidence · week two

One number, 0 to 1, that actually predicts whether the map is right.

Passes
on a deliberately hard track, confidence falls
Trap
The correction interface ranks by confidence, so a model that is confidently wrong is worse than one that abstains. A calibration curve is worth more here than another point of F-measure.

7 · What to reach for

Beats, downbeatsmadmom, Beat This, or a recent neural tracker Compare at least two — they fail differently, and the disagreement is itself a confidence signal
FormBeat-synchronous chroma and MFCC, then a self-similarity matrix Beat-synchronous, not fixed-window. This is what makes it tempo-invariant
EmbeddingsMERT (95M, then 330M)A BERT for music. Freeze it, train a small head
Metricsmir_evalCross-check our bench against it once, early. If they disagree, our bench is wrong
Labelled dataHarmonix Set (~900 songs), SALAMI (~1,400) Beat, downbeat and segment annotations. Labels only, no audio — by design
Audio you may keepMTG-Jamendo (55k full CC tracks), Free Music Archive Clean licence. Use for anything we train on

On sourcing audio, since it will come up in the first hour. Streaming accounts cannot give us samples, and this is settled rather than merely difficult: Spotify playback is decrypted inside the browser's DRM module where no script can reach it, and their beat and section analysis closed to new applications in November 2024. What streaming can give is identity and position — which recording, and where in it. That is enough for a renderer and useless for a listener. So: local files you own for analysis, CC catalogues for training, and a fingerprint (Chromaprint / AcoustID) to key the map to a recording. A map is an annotation, not a recording, which is what makes the corpus distributable even when the songs are not.
