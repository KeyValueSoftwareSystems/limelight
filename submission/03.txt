The primary user is the person responsible for lights who is not a lighting designer.

The first group we would target is mobile and bedroom DJs, and small-venue and event operators - people who already own lights and have nobody to run them. They are specific and reachable. They own a few DMX pars, maybe a moving head, and a laptop. They already spend hours programming a set by hand, or they give up and use sound-active mode. They gather in places we can reach with no industry relationship at all: DJ subreddits, Discord servers, Facebook groups, YouTube gear channels, and the retailers who sold them the fixtures.

We pick them first for one reason. They already pay to solve this problem, so willingness to pay is demonstrated rather than assumed. Our job is to be better than what they buy today, not to create a category.

The second wave is people with smart lights at home - Hue, WLED, Govee, Nanoleaf. That audience is far larger, and reaching it needs no new work from us, because it is the same file with a different reader. That matters as evidence as much as revenue: if one file drives a professional rig and a domestic light strip without being rewritten, the platform claim is true rather than asserted.

Third, and later, are the companies that ship music-reactive features themselves - video editors, fitness apps, game studios, VJ and DJ software. Each of them builds and maintains its own weaker version of this. They are a licensing market, not a starting point.
